How many hours of wedding video coverage do you need?

The useful answer is not a package number. It is the window that holds the beginning, middle, and ending you want to remember.

Begin with the scenes, not the number.

Wedding video coverage is often talked about in hours first: six, eight, ten, or more. Hours matter, of course. But choosing them before you know what you want filmed can turn a meaningful decision into a guess.

A better question is: what would you miss if it were not there? For some couples, that means the private letters, the room full of people getting ready, the ceremony words, the toasts, and the final chaotic dance. For others, it means arriving close to the ceremony and staying through the first hour of the reception. Neither is more correct. The coverage window should reflect the actual shape of your day.

What each part of the day adds.

Getting-ready coverage gives a film its beginning. It can hold the anticipation, the people helping you get dressed, a note read aloud, or the quiet moment before the day gets loud. It does not need to be hours of staged detail shots; even a focused window can give the story context.

The ceremony is usually non-negotiable, but consider the sound as carefully as the image. Vows, readings, and the person officiating are often what turn a beautiful sequence into a personal film. Make sure your videographer has a clear plan for microphones and the ceremony location.

The reception is where your community shows itself. Toasts, parent dances, a packed dance floor, a cultural tradition, or a private last dance are not interchangeable. Decide which of those are part of the memory you want to preserve, then use the timeline to make sure coverage reaches them.

Common coverage shapes.

These are planning patterns, not packages or promises. Travel between locations, ceremonies with multiple traditions, and venue logistics can all change the actual window.

  • Focused coverage: arriving shortly before the ceremony and staying through the key reception events. This can fit a day where the ceremony and formal reception moments are the center of the story.
  • Full-day story: a beginning with getting ready or a first look, the ceremony, portraits, cocktail hour, and a substantial part of the reception. This creates room for the day to unfold rather than treating it as a sequence of appointments.
  • Late ending: extending through a final dance, exit, or distinctive party tradition. This matters when the ending is genuinely part of your wedding—not just a logistical finish line.

If you are unsure, place your ceremony start and reception end on paper, then mark the moments you care about. The gap between those markers is often a much clearer first answer than a generic coverage tier.

Protect the words and the transitions.

Some of the most valuable things in a wedding film cannot be recreated later: vows said in your own voice, a toast from someone you love, a parent whispering before a dance. Let your videographer know about every meaningful speaking moment, including private letters, readings, prayers, and surprise speeches. Sound needs a plan.

Also account for transitions. Moving between a hotel and venue, walking from ceremony to cocktail hour, finding family members for photos, or waiting for a grand entrance each consumes time. A thoughtful schedule does not pretend those minutes do not exist. It puts enough air around them that nobody has to sprint through the parts that are supposed to feel good.

Questions that make the decision easier.

  • Do we want to remember the beginning of the day, or does the story really start at the ceremony for us?
  • Which spoken moments would we be sad not to hear again?
  • Are there cultural events, multiple locations, or travel time that change the normal flow?
  • Does the final dance, exit, or late-night energy matter to us—or would we rather use that coverage earlier?
  • What time do the formal reception events actually happen, not just when does the reception end?

Give your videographer the most current timeline, including addresses and the order of formalities. That makes it possible to shape coverage around the day you are actually having, not an old draft of it.

Choose the coverage that lets you be present.

The right wedding video coverage does more than check off events. It creates enough space to notice your people, hear the words being said, and let the day move at a human pace. That is where the moments that feel most like you tend to live.
