A wedding video timeline template: what happens, and when.

A timeline works best when it is a map, not a minute-by-minute test. Use this outline to protect the parts of your day you want to keep.

Build around moments, then add the clock.

A helpful wedding timeline starts with the scenes that matter to you: getting ready with your people, a letter, a first look, a ceremony, a few minutes together after, a toast, a dance, a final song. Once you know those scenes, place them in order and give each one enough breathing room to happen naturally.

This is not a universal schedule. It is a planning template you can adapt with your planner, venue, photographer, and video team. Add drive time, hair and makeup completion, cultural events, meal timing, and anything else that belongs to your actual day.

A flexible wedding video timeline outline.

  • Getting ready: Details, the people around you, final touches, letters, or gifts. Decide whether you want this quiet beginning in your film.
  • First look or pre-ceremony portraits: If you choose one, this is a useful window for couple, wedding-party, and some family photos before guests arrive.
  • Ceremony: Include a short buffer before guests enter. Confirm the audio plan for vows, readings, and officiant remarks.
  • Just married: Leave a few minutes for hugs, a private breath, and the transition out of the ceremony. This is often more meaningful than it looks on paper.
  • Family photos and portraits: Decide who needs to be there, where they will meet, and how much time you want to be alone together. Work backward from sunset for outdoor portraits.
  • Reception entrance and toasts: Confirm the order with the coordinator or DJ. A single, accurate timeline helps every vendor know when to be ready.
  • Dances, open floor, and ending: Choose whether the film needs the final dance or exit, or whether the story is complete earlier in the reception.

Leave buffers where life happens.

The most useful timeline blocks are often the ones without a formal name: the walk between spaces, five minutes after family photos, the pause before an entrance, a chance to drink water. Small buffers are not wasted time. They keep the people in the schedule from feeling like they are always behind it.

They also give photo and video teams room to catch what cannot be scheduled: a hug from a grandparent, your friends laughing before the reception opens, the shift in the room after you have said your vows.

Share one final version.

About a week before the wedding, send the latest timeline to your videographer. Include addresses, names for key speakers, ceremony and reception start times, vendor contacts, and any surprise moments. If the day changes, tell the team; no timeline survives perfectly, and a good crew expects to adapt.
