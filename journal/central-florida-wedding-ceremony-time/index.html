What time should a Central Florida wedding ceremony start?

The best answer is not a fixed clock time. It starts with sunset, then makes room for the people and moments that happen after the ceremony.

Start at the end of the day.

When couples ask what time their Central Florida wedding ceremony should begin, it is tempting to answer with a number: 4:30, 5:00, 5:30. But the useful answer changes with the season, the ceremony length, the venue, and what you want to do once you walk back down the aisle.

A better starting point is sunset. Work backward from the light you want for portraits, then leave space for a ceremony, hugs, family photos, and the completely normal reality that people take a little time to get where they are going.

That approach matters in Orlando, Winter Park, and across Central Florida, where the sun can feel generous one minute and be gone behind trees, buildings, or a summer storm the next. The aim is not to force the day into a perfect golden-hour photo shoot. It is to protect an unhurried pocket of time where you can actually be together.

Use sunset as the anchor—not the ceremony itself.

For an outdoor ceremony with portraits afterward, a simple starting framework looks like this:

  • Set aside roughly 45 to 75 minutes before sunset for couple portraits, depending on travel, the size of your wedding party, and how much portrait time matters to you.
  • Put family photos and wedding-party photos either before the ceremony, immediately after it, or partly on both sides—rather than assuming they will somehow fit into a tiny gap.
  • Work backward for your ceremony length and a little breathing room at the exit. A 30-minute ceremony rarely means you are free exactly 30 minutes later.

For example, if sunset is at 7:30 p.m. and you want to begin relaxed portraits around 6:45, a 5:45 or 6:00 ceremony might make sense for a short ceremony with many photos handled beforehand. A 4:45 ceremony may be more realistic if you want a longer ceremony, a full set of family photos after, or an unhurried cocktail hour transition. The point is not that either time is universally right; it is that the desired light and pace lead the schedule.

Why a few minutes of margin changes the film.

A wedding film is not only made of the formal milestones. It is built from the glances before a first look, the way a parent reaches for your hand after the ceremony, the exhale when you finally step away together, and the voices around you as the reception begins.

When every one of those scenes has to happen in a three-minute sprint, the camera can still document it—but the experience changes. A little margin gives you the chance to let the moment happen instead of performing it for a schedule. That is especially true if the film you want is grounded and personal rather than a fast highlight reel.

Central Florida timing has a few extra variables.

Sunset is only one piece of usable light. An indoor ceremony may need a different plan because the room is naturally darker. A garden ceremony surrounded by tall trees can lose direct light well before the official sunset. Downtown locations and venues with covered courtyards can do the same. In summer, heat and afternoon rain might make an earlier first look or a flexible portrait window feel kinder to everyone.

That is why a venue walk-through and a conversation with your planner and photo/video team are worth more than a generic rule. They know where the sun lands, where people will gather, how far the rooms are apart, and whether a five-minute golf-cart ride belongs in the schedule.

Three questions to ask before you set the ceremony time.

  • Do we want to see each other before the ceremony? A first look can move many portraits earlier and make the post-ceremony window much easier.
  • Which people and moments matter most after the ceremony? Family photos, a private moment, cocktail hour, a sunset walk, and a room reveal all need real minutes, not just a label on a timeline.
  • What does our venue’s light actually do? Ask where the sun will be during your date and season, and what areas stay usable when weather shifts.

Once you have those answers, choose a timeline that leaves a little room to be humans in it. Your day will feel better, and the images and film will carry more of the feeling that made it yours.
